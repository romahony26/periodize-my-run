# Running principles

The training principles Periodize is built on, written in the project's own words.

This document contains ideas, findings and rules, not anyone's text, tables or training schedules. Where a principle rests on
published research, the study is named so it can be checked. Where it is a widely used coaching convention, or simply a choice
this project made, it says so. Nothing here is medical advice.

Each principle is marked:

- **[R]** a finding from peer-reviewed research
- **[C]** a common coaching convention, not established by controlled studies
- **[P]** this project's own choice of value or rule

## 1. What decides how fast you race

1. **Three things set distance-running performance: aerobic capacity, the fraction of it you can sustain, and how economically
   you run.** [R] Training moves all three, at different speeds. (Joyner and Coyle, 2008; Bassett and Howley, 2000)
2. **Threshold pace is the most useful single number.** [C] It is roughly the pace you could hold for an hour. Training paces
   are set as fractions of it, and it tracks race performance from 10K to the marathon.
3. **Fitness is read from what you actually ran, not from your goal.** [P] Paces follow evidence: best recent efforts, recent
   races, and your own pace-to-heart-rate relationship. A goal time only shows the gap.
4. **Fitness estimates move slowly.** [P] The threshold estimate may rise by at most 1.5% a week and fall by at most 0.5%, so
   one good or bad run cannot swing the plan.
5. **Longer races need endurance on top of speed.** [C] A marathon prediction from a 10K time assumes the long runs, weekly
   volume and race-pace running are in place. Until they are, the prediction is slowed.

## 2. How hard, and how often

6. **Most running is easy.** [R] Successful endurance athletes do roughly four fifths of their training at low intensity, with
   a small share of hard work. (Seiler, 2010; Stöggl and Sperlich, 2014)
7. **Easy means easy.** [R] The best-documented training error is running easy days harder than intended and hard days easier.
   (Foster and colleagues, 2001)
8. **Hard days are separated by easy days.** [C] No two sessions back to back; the long run counts as a hard day.
9. **A week has one long run and one or two sessions.** [C] The rest is easy running or rest.
10. **A missed session is moved only if it fits.** [P] It goes to a later day that keeps hard days apart. If none exists it is
    dropped: squeezing sessions together costs more than the session gains.

## 3. Building volume

11. **Volume is built before intensity, and general work before race-specific work.** [C]
12. **Load rises in steps, with lighter weeks.** [C] Three building weeks are followed by an easier one.
13. **Your own history sets your limits.** [P] Peak weekly distance and long-run length come from what you have handled before,
    not from a table.
14. **A single long run should not jump.** [R] A run more than 10% longer than your longest of the previous 30 days is linked
    with more overuse injuries. The same study found no link with week-to-week mileage change. (Frandsen and colleagues, 2025)
15. **Everyday walking is load too.** [P] Unusually heavy weeks on your feet count as a warning sign for the weekly plan.

## 4. The sessions

16. **Threshold running** raises the pace you can sustain. [C] It is done in repeats with short recoveries, and progresses by
    adding time at the pace before raising the pace.
17. **Faster repeats** (around 10K and 5K pace) develop aerobic capacity. [R] (Midgley and colleagues, 2006)
18. **Race-pace running** becomes the centre of training in the final phase before a long race. [C]
19. **Aerobic runs by heart rate.** [C] Before the race-specific phase, one run a week is held at a fixed heart rate below
    threshold. If the heart rate climbs, you slow down. The heart rate is raised only when 10 miles at the current level is
    comfortable.
20. **Short fast strides** keep speed and running form without fatigue. [C]
21. **Strength training improves running economy.** [R] (Blagrove and colleagues, 2018)

## 5. Is it working?

22. **Faster at the same heart rate is the sign of aerobic progress.** [C] Periodize tracks your pace at fixed heart rates month
    by month, and offers a stepped heart-rate test about every month.
23. **Aerobic change is slow.** [C] Expect little after three weeks and clear change after six. Testing more often measures noise.
24. **Hills are converted to their flat equivalent.** [R] The energy cost of running on a gradient has been measured, and is
    used to compare hilly and flat runs. (Minetti and colleagues, 2002)
25. **Weeks are compared, not single runs.** [P] One run's pace for its heart rate swings with wind, heat, route and time of day.
26. **Race paces should spread predictably with distance.** [C] A well-trained runner slows by about 16 seconds a mile each
    time the distance doubles. A wider spread means endurance, not speed, is the limit.
27. **Aerobic drift.** [C] On a long steady run, how much pace per heartbeat fades in the second half. Under 5% is a common
    rule of thumb.

## 6. Training load

28. **Load is minutes weighted by intensity.** [R] Easy, moderate and hard minutes count 1, 2 and 3. (Lucia and colleagues, 2003)
29. **Fitness and fatigue are both responses to load, on different time scales.** [R] Fitness builds and fades over weeks,
    fatigue over days; form is the difference. (Banister, 1991) The 42-day and 7-day constants are convention. [C]
30. **Execution is measured against the plan.** [P] A run is scored on how far its easy, moderate and hard minutes departed from
    the session set. Too much counts the same as too little.

## 7. Recovery

31. **Sleep matters from a single night.** [R] One short night measurably reduces endurance performance the next day, mostly
    through how hard the effort feels. (Lopes and colleagues, 2023; Craven and colleagues, 2022)
32. **Heart-rate variability and resting heart rate are read as trends.** [R] A single reading is noisy. A 7-day average
    against your own normal range is the established method, and a shift of about half your normal spread is the smallest
    change treated as real. (Plews and colleagues, 2012, 2013)
33. **Everything is judged against your own normal.** [R] Sleep need and heart-rate variability are individual. (Walsh and
    colleagues, 2021)
34. **Poor recovery eases the fast running, not the easy running.** [P] Each warning sign slows the day's fast paces by a small
    fixed amount, learned from your own runs once there are enough.
35. **Illness and injury mean rest, then a gradual return.** [C]

## 8. Racing

36. **Taper by cutting volume, not intensity.** [R] Reducing volume by roughly half over about two weeks while keeping some
    fast running improves performance. (Bosquet and colleagues, 2007)
37. **Even effort is the fastest way to cover a course.** [C] On a hilly course that means slower uphill and faster downhill.
38. **Fuel long efforts.** [R] About 30 g of carbohydrate an hour for 1 to 2 hours, up to 60 g for 2 to 3 hours, and up to 90 g
    beyond that if practised. (Jeukendrup, 2014)
39. **Practise fuelling in training.** [C] The amount is stepped up over the build toward race day.
40. **Hilly races need hilly training.** [C] Weekly climb is raised toward the race's climb per mile over the final months.
41. **Race results are compared across ages with age grading.** [R] Standards by age, sex and distance are public-domain
    tables. (Alan Jones, 2025)
42. **A race is followed by recovery.** [C] Easy running before any session returns.

## 9. Measurement limits

43. **Wrist heart rate is imperfect.** [R] It is least reliable at high intensity and in the cold, so heart-rate-based
    measures are used over minutes, not seconds.
44. **A watch's distance on a treadmill is a guess.** [P] Treadmill runs are judged by heart rate and time.
45. **A watch's altitude can be wrong.** [P] Each watch's altitude is checked on runs that start and finish in the same place,
    and hills are only adjusted for when it proves reliable.
46. **Every score here is an estimate.** [P] Freshness, form, the execution score and the forecasts are this project's own
    measures built from watch data. They guide training; they do not diagnose anything.
